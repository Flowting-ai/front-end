# 7 Product Team Pitfalls You Should Avoid

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/product-team-pitfalls

## Overview
Unlike the other case studies in this collection, this article is not a single-product teardown — it's a synthesis drawn from research with roughly **25,000 Growth.Design subscribers**, identifying seven recurring mistakes product teams make between deciding to build a feature and actually shipping it. It's structured as a short interactive experience (described as "4 minutes") walking through each pitfall with a dialogue-style example, the underlying psychological concept, and a suggested countermeasure. It's a useful reference for process and team-behavior issues rather than any single interface.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Copying What Other Companies Do
Teams often try to replicate a competitor's successful feature (the example given: "Let's do like the new TikTok feed") without understanding *why* it worked for that competitor. The countermeasure is **first-principles thinking**: understanding the root cause of why a pattern works (or doesn't) before adapting it, rather than copying its surface form. Without that underlying psychological understanding, a copied feature tends to be superficial and ineffective in a different product context.

### 2. Relying Heavily on "Instinct"
This pitfall is illustrated by a team member skipping user research entirely, saying: "No time to waste on user research! I KNOW this will work... I feel it in my gut!" The underlying issue is that products exist to solve *someone else's* problem, so decisions must be grounded in actual user understanding rather than internal assumption. The article invokes the maxim "the truth is outside the building" — instincts alone cannot substitute for external validation when building something people will genuinely want.

### 3. Not Testing Ideas Early
The example describes a team spending **11 months of hard work** building a feature in isolation before ever showing it to users — "building behind closed doors is a huge risk nowadays." The article prescribes three minimal resources needed for early testing: (1) **Wireframes** — "you don't even need colors" to get useful feedback; (2) **Fans** — people who already believe in the product's mission are more willing to give honest, engaged feedback; and (3) **Phone** — the simplest, most underused tool for getting quick input is literally calling a user. Testing early with these low-cost resources "could save your business precious time and tons of money," in contrast to the 11-month blind-build scenario.

### 4. ONLY Doing What the BOSS Wants
Illustrated by an executive demanding, "Can we add another call-to-action on top to make it POP?" even though the design is already well-balanced. This is named the **"CEO Button"** — an unusual executive request that destroys the balance of an otherwise sound design — and is linked to the **Dunning-Kruger Effect**, where people (in this case, executives without direct exposure to the underlying user research) overestimate their expertise on decisions outside their domain. The article recommends two countermeasures: **Trust** (reminding stakeholders they hired the product team for a reason) and **Vocabulary** (learning the psychology behind design decisions so the team has the specific language to justify pushing back). It closes the section with a Tim Ferriss quote: "Success can be measured by the number of hard discussions you're willing to have."

### 5. Compromising to Make Everyone Happy
Shown through a team accepting every stakeholder request in a single round (adding a filter, more illustrations, and an activity feed all at once), resulting in a diluted, "watered-down" solution where "users are lost because of noise." The recommended discipline is **filtering feedback** against a clear, previously-established purpose/problem statement — that clarity makes it far easier to evaluate which pieces of feedback should actually be incorporated. Importantly, the article stresses balance: "feedback is what takes your product to the next level. Don't dismiss it because it doesn't please you" — the fix is selective incorporation, not wholesale rejection of stakeholder input.

### 6. Force Your Beliefs on Others
Illustrated by a team member forwarding an article to colleagues because it "confirms my idea!" without weighing any contrary evidence. This is a direct example of **confirmation bias** — the tendency to favor and use information that supports what we already believe. The article's advice is to explicitly explore both sides of an argument before sharing supporting material with colleagues, warning that one-sided advocacy will cause colleagues to tune the person out ("if not, they'll ignore you"), damaging both the argument's credibility and the team's trust in that person's judgment going forward.

### 7. Having No Idea Why Something Feels Odd
Depicted by a team throwing out random, ungrounded fixes when something feels off ("Should we add onboarding steps? ...hmm or add some padding... ...Maybe do an A/B test?") without diagnosing the actual problem first. The article frames the fix as a matter of experience: **"skills come with repetitions"** — the more design/product situations a person has been exposed to, the better they become at recognizing patterns and predicting what's likely to go wrong before jumping to solutions. This is illustrated with the analogy of chess grandmaster Garry Kasparov competing against Deep Blue, a computer that "thinks 10 million times faster than him" — Kasparov's pattern recognition from thousands of prior games lets him evaluate positions intuitively, in the same way accumulated product/design exposure builds the ability to diagnose issues before reaching for random fixes.

## Key Takeaways
- Copying a competitor's feature without understanding the underlying psychology behind it usually fails — dig for the first-principles reason it works before adapting it.
- Gut instinct is not a substitute for direct user contact; the truth about what will work lives with users, not inside the building.
- Cheap, fast tests (wireframes, engaged "fans," and phone calls) done early can prevent months of wasted, isolated development effort.
- Executive requests that seem to come from confidence rather than user data (the "CEO Button") are best handled with earned trust and a well-articulated psychological rationale, not silent compliance.
- Trying to satisfy every stakeholder's feedback at once dilutes a product; filter feedback against a clearly stated problem/purpose instead of accepting or rejecting it wholesale.
- One-sided advocacy (confirmation bias) erodes team trust — always weigh contrary evidence before pushing a position onto colleagues.
- Diagnostic intuition for "something feels off" is built through repeated exposure to many product situations, not shortcuts — invest in pattern-recognition experience over time.

## Applicability Notes
This case study applies less to any specific UI or flow and more to internal product-team process, stakeholder management, and decision-making culture. It's most useful as a reference during roadmap planning, design reviews, or when a team is negotiating scope with executives or reconciling conflicting stakeholder feedback — situations where the risk is organizational/process failure rather than a specific interface flaw.
