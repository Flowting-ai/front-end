# How To Properly Apply Jobs-To-Be-Done To User Onboarding

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/headspace-user-onboarding

## Overview
This case study follows a stressed narrator who downloads Headspace (and later Calm) looking for quick relief through meditation, and uses their onboarding journey to demonstrate how the Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) framework is frequently misapplied. Rather than being a simple teardown, it's built around the idea that correctly identifying a customer's underlying "job" is necessary but not sufficient — the framing, sequencing, and timing of every subsequent onboarding interaction can either reinforce or undermine that job. It's a useful reference for any onboarding flow that combines goal-selection, personalization, and a paywall.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Loading Time = Opportunities
Loading screens and spinners are typically wasted, ambiguity-inducing moments, but they can instead be used to deliver value before asking for any commitment. Headspace's splash screen plays a calming meditation-themed animation while the app loads, rather than a generic spinner. This primes the user's psychological state — helping them transition away from their current stressed mindset — before any signup friction is introduced. The narrator reacts positively in the moment ("Great start! Helping me break free of my current thoughts"), showing the technique working as intended.

### 2. Framing Customers' Jobs
A Job-to-be-Done is defined as wanting to change an existing situation into a preferred one, and how that job is framed in copy directly affects whether users feel understood. After login, Headspace asks users why they want to meditate, offering options like "Manage my anxiety," "Sleep soundly," and "Improve focus." The case study flags that anxiety-related options are framed *negatively* (avoidance-oriented: "Manage my anxiety") rather than *positively* (aspiration-oriented, e.g., "Feel more calm"), and asks rhetorically whether users would ever say "Avoid nightmares" instead of "Sleep soundly" — probably not. The narrator's actual job ("I mostly just want to relax and be grateful") wasn't represented among the offered options at all, creating an immediate mismatch between the tool's framing and the user's real motivation. The recommended fix is researching actual customer jobs directly (asking "why do you want to hire us?") and phrasing options around positive aspirations rather than problems to avoid.

### 3. Labor Illusion
People trust and value a result more when it's delivered after a perceptible, even artificial, delay — a "carefully crafted" wait signals effort. The case study observes that Headspace jumps directly from the job-selection screen to a list of course options with no transitional moment, and argues Headspace could instead show an animated "customizing your plan" screen between the two steps. This staged delay would create the perception of tailored effort and elevate the perceived value of the resulting course recommendations. The article notes this idea is drawn from Growth.Design's own "BIAS Framework."

### 4. Endowment Effect
Users place higher value on things they feel are personally theirs, and personalization at first use measurably lifts activation. At the paywall (described as occurring at "Slide 10"), Headspace shows generic subscription tiers with no reference back to the goal the user selected moments earlier (in this case, "Manage anxiety"). The case study argues that framing the offer as "Your personalized plan for anxiety relief" would trigger ownership feelings over a tailored solution, rather than presenting an undifferentiated commercial ask. The mismatch nearly causes the narrator to abandon the app at this exact point ("I almost dropped here... but I'm somewhat already committed").

### 5. Reciprocity
People feel obligated to give something back once they've received value, but asking for multiple favors in a row — before any value has been delivered — violates this norm. Headspace's flow asks users to (1) create an account with email/password, (2) select a goal, (3) decide on a subscription, and (4) grant notification permissions — all before the user has completed a single meditation session. The case study argues notification permission requests, in particular, should be deferred until *after* the first meditation session, when the user has experienced tangible value and is more naturally inclined to reciprocate. As the narrator puts it, "the timing of asking for notifications is really off here... even if they say WHY I need them" — meaning good justificatory copy cannot fully compensate for bad timing.

### 6. Consider User Psych (Fogg Behavior Model Context)
Referencing BJ Fogg's Behavior Model (motivation + ability + prompt = behavior), the case study argues that asking for information or payment only works reliably when user motivation is high; when both motivation and ability have been depleted by prior friction, the same prompt fails. By the time the narrator tries a second meditation app (Calm) later in the journey, their psychological reserves ("psych") have already been worn down by Headspace's friction, so Calm's nearly identical paywall-before-value pattern causes an outright drop-off. The stated insight: "if you craft every interaction with the intention of giving value first, you can overcome the worst context" — i.e., value-first sequencing can compensate for otherwise unfavorable circumstances.

## Key Takeaways
- Correctly identifying the customer's Job-to-be-Done is necessary but not sufficient — how that job is framed in copy (positive/aspirational vs. negative/avoidant) determines whether users feel understood.
- Loading and transition moments are opportunities to deliver value or build perceived effort (Labor Illusion), not just technical necessities to be minimized.
- Personalizing paywalls and offers around the user's previously stated goal activates the Endowment Effect and reduces drop-off at the highest-friction point in onboarding.
- Sequencing matters: stacking multiple "asks" (signup, payment, permissions) back-to-back without any intervening value delivery violates reciprocity norms and depletes user motivation.
- Notification and permission requests land far better after a user has experienced a tangible "win" than immediately post-signup.
- Cumulative friction across an onboarding flow lowers a user's "psych" (motivation/ability) such that even well-justified requests later in the flow are more likely to fail.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most directly applicable to onboarding flows that combine a goal/preference-selection step with a subscription paywall — common in wellness, habit-forming, and subscription-based mobile apps. It's also a useful reference for any flow that stacks multiple sequential "asks" (account creation, personalization questions, payment, permissions) and needs guidance on ordering those asks relative to actual value delivery.
