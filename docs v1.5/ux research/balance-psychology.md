# The Psychology of Advertising: Why this ad made me stop scrolling

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/balance-psychology

## Overview
This case study dissects why a particular ad for the meditation app Balance managed to stop a scrolling user in their tracks, when the vast majority of ads get filtered out and ignored. The author encountered an ad styled to look like an Apple Notes screenshot and breaks down the specific psychological mechanisms that made it effective, then critiques where the ad went too far into cleverness at the expense of clarity, proposes a redesign, and finally tracks how Balance's advertising evolved afterward (into a testimonial-style ad) with further critique. Unlike most case studies in this series, this one cites no hard conversion metrics — it is a purely qualitative walkthrough of advertising psychology, useful as a reference for marketing/ad creative and attention-capture design generally, not just product UI.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Familiarity Bias
The psychology: people process and engage more comfortably with things that resemble something they already know well; recognizing a familiar pattern lowers cognitive resistance and can prime a more receptive mental state.

Implementation: the Balance ad was designed to visually mimic the Apple Notes app interface — the same plain, personal, text-on-white-background format users see when reviewing their own private notes. By adopting this familiar visual language, the ad triggers the same relaxed, attentive mental mode users enter when reading something written by/for themselves, rather than the defensive mode triggered by obvious ad creative.

### 2. Von Restorff Effect (Isolation Effect)
The psychology: when several similar items appear together, the one item that differs from the rest is disproportionately noticed and remembered — distinctiveness relative to surrounding context drives attention.

Implementation: on a social feed dominated by photo and video content, a plain text-based "note" stands out precisely because it looks nothing like the surrounding image-heavy posts. The ad's format becomes the visual outlier in the feed, which is what earns the stopped scroll. The case study frames this as a deliberate strategy of reading the platform context and choosing a creative format that contrasts with it.

### 3. Attentional Bias
The psychology: people pay more attention to information that already occupies their mental space or matches a concern they're already primed with — we notice what we're already thinking about.

Implementation: the ad's copy addresses wasting time on social media, a behavior/anxiety the target user is often already consciously aware of in that very moment (since they are, in fact, scrolling social media). This alignment between the ad's message and the user's present mental state increases the odds the message lands. The case study frames this as a broader tip: understand the moments when your customer most needs your product, and design messaging/timing around those moments.

### 4. Informative vs. Creative Balance (Clarity Over Cleverness)
The psychology, via David Ogilvy's dictum: "I don't want you to say my ad was creative. I want you to find it so interesting, you want to buy the product." Cleverness that obscures the actual offering can backfire.

Implementation/critique: the original Balance ad was judged "too clever" — its Apple Notes mimicry was so convincing that some users reportedly mistook it for a Google product and missed which app was actually being advertised, along with other key details. The case study uses this as a cautionary example: excessive cleverness in creative execution risks the audience misreading or entirely missing the intended message, and can edge into feeling deceptive.

### 5. Customer Fatigue (Novelty Decay / Kano Model)
The psychology: tactics that delight the first time lose their power with repeated exposure — the Kano Model describes how today's "delighter" features become tomorrow's baseline expectation, requiring continuous innovation to keep having impact.

Implementation/context: Balance's sister app, Elevate (same parent company), used the identical Apple Notes-style ad format. Reusing the same attention-grabbing device across products/campaigns risks the audience becoming desensitized to it faster, since the "surprise" of the familiar-yet-out-of-place format wears off with repetition. The case study uses this to argue that ad creative needs to keep evolving rather than reusing a winning format indefinitely.

### 6. Pseudo-Set Framing (Proposed Redesign)
The psychology: presenting content as a numbered/itemized set (e.g., a checklist or numbered list) increases people's motivation to complete the full set — once someone starts reading item 1, the implied set nudges them to continue rather than stop.

Implementation (redesign proposal): the improved ad concept formats its message as a set of numbered tasks/points rather than a single block of prose, which is intended to prevent readers from disengaging after just the first line.

### 7. Framing (Word Choice) (Proposed Redesign)
The psychology: the specific words used to describe a benefit shape how the offering is mentally categorized and associated, independent of the underlying facts.

Implementation (redesign proposal): the redesign swaps language centered on "brain" benefits for language centered on "self-care," which the case study argues aligns more accurately with how meditation is actually positioned and experienced by users, producing better mental associations with the product.

### 8. Contrast (Proposed Redesign)
The psychology: visually emphasized elements against a lower-contrast background draw the eye and communicate importance/hierarchy.

Implementation (redesign proposal): the call-to-action and the company/app name are given stronger visual emphasis (contrast) in the redesigned ad, so the two most important actionable pieces of information are not lost inside the note-styled body text.

### 9. Spark Effect (Proposed Redesign)
The psychology: motivation to begin a behavior increases sharply when the very first required action is made extremely easy, with a clear, achievable goal — removing the activation-energy barrier to starting.

Implementation (redesign proposal): the redesign offers an easy, low-effort initial task with an explicit, clear goal, intended to give the user a frictionless way to begin engaging (e.g., starting the app/experience) rather than presenting a vague or effortful first step.

### 10. Testimonial Authenticity (Follow-up Ad Critique)
The psychology, again via Ogilvy: testimonials that are too polished make audiences suspect the "customer" is a paid actor, undermining the credibility testimonials are supposed to provide; and mentioning competitor names in a testimonial can create confusing associations rather than reinforcing the advertiser's brand.

Implementation/critique: after the numbered/reframed ad ran for some time, Balance shifted its creative to a testimonial-style ad quoting a customer review. The case study critiques this iteration for being too polished (risking the "obviously an actor" perception) and flags the risk of naming competitors within the testimonial copy, recommending more authentic, less-produced-sounding testimonials instead.

## Key Takeaways
- Mimicking a familiar, trusted interface (like a personal notes app) can lower a viewer's guard and prime a more receptive attention state.
- Standing out from the surrounding visual context (Von Restorff effect) is often more effective at stopping scroll than being intrinsically "prettier."
- Message timing/framing that matches what the audience is already thinking about (attentional bias) increases relevance and impact.
- Cleverness has a ceiling: if an ad is so clever that people misidentify the brand or the offer, the cleverness has become a liability, not an asset.
- Winning creative tactics decay with repeated or shared use across a company's own products — plan for creative refresh rather than reusing a working format indefinitely.
- Testimonials should read as authentic and unpolished, and should avoid referencing competitors, to preserve credibility.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to ad creative and marketing/growth teams designing scroll-stopping social ads, but its underlying attention and framing principles (familiarity, contrast/distinctiveness, benefit framing, spark effect, authenticity in social proof) generalize usefully to any first-touch marketing surface, landing page hero section, or onboarding screen that needs to capture attention and communicate value within a few seconds.
