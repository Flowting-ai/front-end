# How Blinkist Increased Trial Conversions by 23% (Ethically)

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/trial-paywall-challenge

## Overview
This case study presents a head-to-head comparison of two paywall/trial-signup screen variants ("Variant A" and "Variant B") for Blinkist, the book-summary subscription app, structured as a challenge where readers are invited to guess which variant performed better and why before the reveal. Variant B produced a 23% lift in trial signups over Variant A, and the article works backward from that result to explain the underlying psychological principles responsible for the difference — notably that Variant B won by removing manipulative/dark-pattern elements and refocusing the screen on customers' actual fears and needs, rather than by adding more persuasive selling points. It closes with a proposed further iteration (Variant C) and a broader ethical argument about sustainable, trust-based growth. This is a valuable reference for any paywall, trial-signup, or subscription-upgrade screen design.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Reactance
The underlying psychology: people resist and push back against a behavior when they feel they are being forced or manipulated into it — perceived coercion triggers an emotional counter-response that can reduce, not increase, compliance.

Blinkist's implementation (Variant A, the losing design): Variant A included a close/dismiss button disguised to look like a back button, effectively hiding the real way to exit the paywall screen. This design made users feel "trapped" rather than free to leave if they weren't ready, and the case study argues this perceived coercion increased resistance to signing up rather than nudging people toward conversion. The stated lesson: "gently nudging a behavior is often better than forcing it."

### 2. Mental Shortcuts (System 1 Thinking)
The underlying psychology: people form fast, subconscious associations based on visual/textual patterns they've seen before, and those associations color their trust judgment even when the actual content is legitimate.

Blinkist's implementation (Variant A): the screen used asterisks paired with tiny, easy-to-miss disclaimer text (e.g., "*Terms and conditions apply"). Even though Blinkist genuinely "had nothing to hide," this visual pattern subconsciously triggered associations with "deceitful TV infomercials" and their fine-print tactics, eroding user trust in the offer despite the terms themselves being transparent and fair.

### 3. Loss Aversion & Customer Pain Points
The underlying psychology: people are more strongly motivated by avoiding a feared loss (e.g., being charged unexpectedly) than by the promise of an equivalent gain — designing around the specific pain/fear a customer has is often more persuasive than listing product benefits.

Blinkist's implementation (Variant B, the winning design): research had identified that the number one reason people cancel trials is "forgetting to cancel the trial and being charged," with 33% of all cancellations happening immediately after the trial starts (i.e., users canceling right away specifically to avoid the risk of a forgotten charge). Rather than listing app features and benefits, Variant B's screen dropped feature-selling entirely and dedicated itself solely to directly addressing this fear — reassuring the user about not getting unexpectedly charged. The case study frames this as prioritizing "avoiding fear and pain" over promoting "hopes and desires."

### 4. Goal-Gradient Effect
The underlying psychology: motivation to complete a process increases as a person perceives themselves getting closer to the finish line; showing visible progress already made (even a small first step) increases the likelihood of continuing.

Proposed enhancement (Variant C, a further iteration beyond A/B): the case study proposes adding a visual progress indicator to the trial timeline showing the "first step" as already completed, so the user perceives their journey as already underway rather than not yet started — intended to leverage the psychological pull of being partway through a process rather than facing a fresh, un-started commitment.

### 5. Transparency as a Trust & Retention Lever
The underlying psychology: proactively delivering on an implicit promise (rather than requiring the user to remember or chase it themselves) builds trust and directly defuses the exact anxiety that drives people to cancel trials early or complain later.

Blinkist's implementation (post-signup flow): immediately after a user confirms payment/starts the trial, Blinkist follows up by offering to notify the user before the trial expires — directly keeping the implicit promise the paywall screen made about not letting them get unexpectedly charged. The measured impact of this transparency-driven follow-up was dramatic: push notification opt-in rates rose from 6% to 74% (a 1,200% increase). This transparency approach also reduced cancellations occurring during the trial period and reduced customer complaints by 55%.

## Core Argument & Conclusion
The case study's central thesis is that Variant B won not by adding more persuasive tactics but by removing manipulative ones (the hidden close button, the fine-print-style disclaimer) and refocusing the screen entirely on the customer's real, research-identified fear. The article distills this into three foundational practices for sustainable conversion growth: (1) start from customers' actual needs, (2) stay transparent and ethical, and (3) base design decisions on psychology rather than guesswork. It concludes that "the only way you can exceed your business goals sustainably is by focusing on delighting your customers first."

## Key Takeaways
- Disguising or hiding an exit path (e.g., a close button styled as a back button) triggers reactance and can hurt conversion rather than help it — users who feel trapped resist more, not less.
- Small visual/textual patterns (tiny asterisked disclaimers) can trigger negative subconscious associations (e.g., "infomercial" cues) regardless of whether the underlying terms are actually fair.
- Directly addressing a well-researched customer fear (e.g., fear of forgotten trial charges) can outperform a features/benefits pitch, especially at a decision point already loaded with anxiety.
- A visible sense of progress (goal-gradient effect) can be used to sustain motivation through a multi-step trial or onboarding journey.
- Proactively delivering on implicit promises (like reminding users before a trial ends) is both an ethical practice and a powerful lever — it drove opt-in rates from 6% to 74% and cut complaints by 55% in this case.
- Ethical, fear-addressing, transparent design outperformed dark-pattern-driven design in head-to-head A/B testing — sustainable growth and short-term manipulation are not the same strategy.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most directly relevant to trial-signup and paywall/upgrade screens for subscription products, particularly around addressing billing-related anxiety and trial-cancellation behavior, but its core lesson — that removing dark patterns and directly addressing a customer's specific, researched fear can outperform benefit-selling — generalizes to any conversion screen where user hesitation stems from distrust or anticipated loss (checkout flows, plan-upgrade prompts, cancellation-flow alternatives).
