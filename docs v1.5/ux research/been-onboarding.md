# How small UI delighters have a huge impact on UX

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/been-onboarding

## Overview
This case study looks at Been, a travel-tracking app whose core value proposition is letting users log every country they've visited and watch their personal "map" of the world fill in. The case study frames the problem as an onboarding flow that fails to motivate users despite a genuinely appealing core concept: the intro screen leads with feature descriptions (widgets, premium features) rather than emotional payoff, and the subsequent flow lacks clear guidance, visual reward, and achievable near-term milestones. It's a useful reference for products whose core loop is "log/track something over time" — travel trackers, habit trackers, collection apps — where the emotional framing and feedback around each small action matters as much as the feature set itself.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Priming
The principle: information presented earlier shapes how people interpret and react to information presented afterward — the same feature can land very differently depending on the emotional or contextual frame set immediately before it. Been's problem is that its intro screen leads with a display of widgets and premium features, priming the user to evaluate the app as a feature list/utility rather than priming them with the emotional meaning of travel-tracking (nostalgia, pride, the joy of reliving trips). The suggested fix is to reframe the introductory content so it establishes emotional meaning and personal relevance *before* revealing what the app can technically do, so subsequent feature reveals land as payoffs rather than a spec sheet.

### 2. Spark Effect
The principle: people are far more likely to take an action when the perceived effort required is small — a big, vague ask stalls people, while a tiny, concrete first step gets them moving. Been's implementation replaces what would otherwise be complex initial guidance with a single, simple opening question, giving users a low-effort entry point into the app rather than a wall of instructions or setup steps. This reduces the initial friction of engagement and gets users into the product's core loop faster.

### 3. Aesthetic Usability
The principle: interfaces that look more polished are perceived — and often actually used — as more effective and easier to use, even when the underlying functionality hasn't changed at all; visual appeal itself increases user confidence and motivation. Been's implementation was simply applying color meaningfully to the interface, which the case study credits with increasing the app's perceived quality and the user's motivation to engage with it, independent of any functional change to the underlying flow.

### 4. Goal-Gradient Effect
The principle: people accelerate their effort the closer they perceive themselves to be to a goal — but the inverse is also true: showing someone how far they are from a huge, distant goal can be actively discouraging rather than motivating. Been's flaw was displaying progress as "0% out of 195 countries" right at the start, which frames the user's accomplishment (having visited any countries at all) as a rounding error against an enormous, distant total. The recommended fix is to remove that distant end-goal framing and instead use "shaping" — reinforcing and celebrating incremental steps (e.g., "you've added your first country!") rather than anchoring users to how far away the finish line is.

### 5. Peak-End Rule
The principle: people don't remember experiences as an average of every moment — they remember them primarily by their most intense point (the peak) and by how they ended, so designing a strong emotional peak into a routine action can disproportionately shape how the whole experience is recalled. Been's implementation creates a delightful, celebratory animation that plays whenever a user adds a new country to their map, turning what could be a mundane data-entry action into a memorable, rewarding moment. The case study credits this small delighter with boosting users' motivation to keep coming back and continuing to populate their map, precisely because that celebratory "peak" moment gets attached to the act of logging travel.

## Additional Issues Addressed
- **Form complexity:** the trip-entry form itself was identified as overly complicated, adding unnecessary friction to the core logging action.
- **Emotional context in data entry:** background imagery evoking travel memories was added during the data-entry step, so that even the "boring" utilitarian task of entering trip details is wrapped in an emotionally resonant visual context rather than presented as a bare form.

## Key Takeaways
- Leading with emotional framing (why this matters to the user) before leading with feature lists changes how the entire rest of the flow is perceived.
- Reducing the very first required action to something small and easy (Spark Effect) meaningfully increases the odds someone starts at all.
- Visual polish (color, imagery) is not just decoration — it measurably changes perceived usability and motivation (Aesthetic Usability).
- Showing users how far they are from a massive end-goal can backfire; celebrate the step just taken instead of the distance still to go (Goal-Gradient Effect used correctly).
- A single well-designed celebratory moment attached to a core action (Peak-End Rule) can disproportionately improve how the whole product is remembered and re-engaged with.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to onboarding flows and core-loop interactions in tracking, logging, or collection-style products (travel trackers, habit trackers, fitness/streak apps, hobby-collection apps) where a single repeatable user action (adding an entry, checking something off, logging a data point) is the heartbeat of the product. The lessons apply broadly anywhere a product needs to turn a small, repetitive input action into something users feel good about doing again.
