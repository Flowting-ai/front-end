# The psychology behind highly effective landing pages

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/landing-page-ux-psychology

## Overview
This case study follows a problem-identification-and-solution structure: it starts by analyzing a poorly designed landing page riddled with common UX failures, identifies the specific psychological/design principles being violated, and then presents a redesigned version that applies those same principles correctly. It's a useful reference for evaluating and improving marketing/conversion landing pages, particularly around visual hierarchy, choice overload, and low-friction calls to action.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Wrongful Delegation
The underlying idea is that product/marketing teams, as the experts on their own offering, have a responsibility to simplify decision-making for visitors rather than dumping every option on them and expecting the user to figure out what matters. The flawed example landing page violated this by including an excessive number of call-to-action buttons — some of which were even repeated more than once on the same page — forcing the visitor to do the work of filtering and prioritizing which action actually mattered. The corrected redesign reduced the number of CTAs down to focus on one single main action, removing the burden of interpretation from the user. The core guidance stated in the case study: "do not delegate the daunting task of filtering/understanding to your audience."

### 2. Law of Proximity
This is the Gestalt principle that elements placed close together are visually perceived as related, while elements spaced apart are perceived as distinct. The problematic version of the landing page had "everything grouped together, so it's hard to understand what's related" — meaning related and unrelated content blocks weren't visually distinguished from each other, making it difficult for users to parse which pieces of information belonged together and which were separate concerns.

### 3. Aesthetics of Reading / Typography Hierarchy
Strong, consistent typography reduces the cognitive load required to scan and understand a page, which in turn improves users' ability to make decisions quickly. The flawed design used many different font sizes, weights, and colors without any clear hierarchy, making it unclear what was most important to read first. The fix involved cleaning up the fonts to create a stronger, clearer visual hierarchy so the eye is guided naturally through the content in order of importance. The case study cites supporting research from MIT showing that good typography helps people perform better on cognitive tasks.

### 4. The Squint Test
The Squint Test is a quick design-evaluation technique: intentionally blur your vision (by slowly closing your eyes or squinting) while looking at a page to see what visual elements still stand out despite the loss of detail — revealing what's actually commanding visual attention versus what should be. Applied to the original flawed landing page, the contrast highlighted the wrong parts of the UI — elements like the top bar, pop-ups, and logos stood out — while the actual core message of the page was effectively invisible under the same blurred test. This diagnostic directly informed the redesign's focus on making the primary message the most visually prominent element.

### 5. Spark Effect
The Spark Effect describes how pairing a very low-friction action with a benefit the user actually wants maximizes the likelihood they'll engage. In the redesign, this was implemented by including a low-friction, low-commitment interactive element — a simple question about the visitor themselves — that required minimal effort to answer. The purpose of this small action was to help users find the right course/offering suited to their specific needs, effectively personalizing the next step without requiring a heavy commitment upfront. The underlying mechanism: people are far more likely to take an action when the required effort is small, so shrinking the first ask increases the odds someone will start engaging at all.

## Specific Issues Identified in the Original (Flawed) Design
- Unanswered basic user questions surfaced during the walkthrough, such as "where is it?!?" and "where do I apply?" — indicating the page failed to answer core navigational questions a visitor would have.
- Crucial information was hidden in fine print rather than surfaced prominently.
- Unclear call-to-action pathways left visitors uncertain about what to do next.
- Overall, the page produced cognitive overload rather than a clear, guided path to action.

## Metrics & Data
No specific conversion percentages or quantified outcome metrics are provided in the case study — the analysis relies entirely on psychological principles, design heuristics (Squint Test, Law of Proximity), and cited supporting research (the MIT typography study) rather than reported before/after performance numbers.

## Key Takeaways
- Don't make visitors do the work of filtering multiple competing CTAs — narrow the page down to one clear primary action.
- Group related content close together and separate unrelated content visually, so structure is legible at a glance (Law of Proximity).
- Establish a clear typographic hierarchy; inconsistent font sizes/weights/colors increase cognitive load and slow comprehension.
- Use the Squint Test as a fast diagnostic to check whether your page's visual emphasis actually matches its intended core message.
- Lower the friction of the very first interaction (e.g., a single easy question) to increase the odds a visitor engages at all before asking for a bigger commitment.
- Fine print and buried information create the exact "where is it?" confusion that a good landing page should eliminate.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to marketing/conversion landing pages, course or product sign-up pages, and any single-purpose page meant to funnel visitors toward one primary action. The typography-hierarchy and Squint Test techniques are also broadly applicable to any UI screen where visual clarity and prioritization of a primary message matter.
