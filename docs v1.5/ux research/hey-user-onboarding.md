# User Onboarding: Is HEY Email Worth It?

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/hey-user-onboarding

## Overview
This case study follows a user who has struggled with email overload (having already failed to fix the problem with Superhuman) and discovers HEY — Basecamp's paid email product — through a founder tweet. The narrative walks through HEY's marketing site, signup flow, and first-run experience to evaluate whether its bold positioning and pricing are backed up by an onboarding experience that actually delivers on its promises. It's a useful reference for products that lead with strong differentiated marketing copy but need their onboarding to convert that promise into an early, tangible "aha" moment.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. When Everybody Zigs, Zag
Differentiation is what allows a brand to rise above competitors offering functionally similar products. HEY's homepage deliberately breaks from typical SaaS landing-page conventions — it lacks a traditional title/hero-image layout and instead leads with a long, text-heavy manifesto that speaks directly to users' pain points with existing email products, rather than making generic feature claims. This positions HEY as fundamentally different from incumbents like Google and Apple by telling an authentic story instead of asserting superiority through feature lists.

### 2. Know Your Customer's Mind Inside Out
Effective copywriting starts from a deep understanding of the customer's dreams, pains, and barriers — not from company-centric language. HEY's homepage is built around pointed quotes that speak directly to the frustrations people have with their inbox (overwhelm, loss of control), rather than describing what the product technically does. Because the copy is anchored in specific, recognizable pain points, it resonates more strongly than a features-first pitch would.

### 3. Endowment Effect
People assign more value to something once they feel it belongs to them, and personalization at first contact measurably lifts activation. On the signup form, HEY pre-fills the user's chosen email address before any other step, giving an immediate sense of ownership — the user describes it as feeling like "it's now mine," despite it being a small interface detail. This early ownership signal is used to build commitment before the harder steps (password, payment) follow.

### 4. Deferred Account Creation
Moving users through a flow gradually — letting them experience a piece of value before formally signing up — increases conversion, and it works via reciprocity: once someone has received something, they feel a pull to give something back (i.e., complete signup). HEY's flow is visually represented as three steps (via hand icons) with the reward (claiming the desired email address) sequenced *before* the friction (creating a password), so password creation feels like a formality after a reward has already been secured. However, the case study identifies a specific flaw: the step counter claims 3 steps but the flow actually contains 4, breaking the promise set at the start — the reviewer warns that "the risk of not meeting people's expectations is greater than adding a small step," i.e., it would have been safer to just label it accurately.

### 5. Contextual Onboarding
Good onboarding shows the right message to the right user at the right time, rather than requiring users to remember instructions given far in advance of when they're needed. After account creation, HEY shows a completion screen listing remaining setup steps, but it redundantly repeats steps already completed and front-loads lengthy explanations of features (like the "Screener") before the user ever encounters them in context. The case study argues this should instead be delivered contextually — for example, explaining the Screener feature when the user's actual first real email arrives, rather than abstractly during setup. It suggests whitelisting HEY's own onboarding email specifically so the Screener feature can be demonstrated live, in-context, on a real incoming message.

### 6. Prioritize Your Aha! Moment
Helping users realize a product's core value as early as possible is critical to onboarding success, and this requires identifying (via data/research) what engaged users commonly do early on, then surfacing those behaviors quickly. HEY's biggest identified gap: new users are shown a flooded inbox immediately, before they've had a chance to experience any of HEY's differentiated core features (Clips, Papertrail, Feed) — these are mentioned by name but never explained or demonstrated in action. Instead of producing an "aha" moment, the flow effectively hands the user an instruction manual and a list of tasks. The case study's suggested fix is to send one onboarding email per day for six days, giving the user a reason to return repeatedly while gradually and contextually revealing each feature, rather than dumping everything (and a full inbox) on the user at once.

## Key Takeaways
- Bold, differentiated marketing copy needs an onboarding experience that lives up to it — otherwise the gap between promise and delivery becomes a source of frustration.
- Small ownership cues (like pre-filling a chosen value on a form) can activate the Endowment Effect cheaply and early in a flow.
- If you tell users how many steps are left, that number must be accurate — an inaccurate step count damages trust more than an honestly-labeled longer flow would.
- Sequencing a small reward before a friction point (deferred account creation) leverages reciprocity to improve completion rates.
- Feature explanations delivered contextually (at the moment of first relevant use) land far better than upfront, abstract explanations delivered before the feature is needed.
- A product's most differentiated features need a fast, deliberate path to a first "aha" moment — dropping users into an overwhelming default state (e.g., a full inbox) before they've experienced core value undermines everything the marketing built up.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to onboarding and first-run flows for productivity, communication, or utility products that carry strong upfront marketing positioning — particularly ones with a "claim your identifier" step (email, username, handle), a multi-screen signup sequence, and several non-obvious differentiated features that need to be introduced progressively rather than all at once.
