# Signal: How To Ethically Boost Your Revenues

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/signal-revenue

## Overview

This case study looks at Signal, the privacy-focused messaging app that relies on donations rather than ads or data sales for revenue. The narrative opens with the author checking Signal during a walk, finding no new messages, and then noticing — for the first time — a donation prompt at the bottom of the screen. The case study uses this moment to examine Signal's donation flow end to end, identifying where good UX fundamentals (like device-based personalization) coexist with missed opportunities (unclear messaging, poor prompt placement, unnecessary decision friction, and a forgettable confirmation). It's a useful reference for how a values-driven product can grow revenue ethically, without resorting to manipulative dark patterns.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. What vs. Why
Telling a user *what* to do (e.g., "donate") without explaining *why* they personally benefit makes the request easy to ignore. Signal's initial donation prompt uses a heart icon and passive wording ("powered by people like me") that state the ask but never clarify the user's stake in it — e.g., that their specific message history and calls stay private *because* of donations like theirs. The case study's underlying principle: "people act when they identify to your why." The recommended fix is to lead with a concrete benefit statement such as "keep messages safe" paired with an explanation of why donations are what make Signal's independence (from ads/data-selling) possible.

### 2. Edge & Banner Blindness
Users have learned to subconsciously ignore content that resembles ads, sits close to ads, or occupies a location traditionally reserved for ads/promotions. Signal's donation prompt is positioned at the bottom edge of the screen — a zone users have trained themselves to tune out. The suggested improvement is to move the prompt toward the center of the screen, the "prime spot for attention," and to combine that repositioning with two other elements: a clear, benefit-first message, and an explanation of why the donation matters to Signal's survival as a company.

### 3. Personalization = Less Friction
Signal already does one thing well: it detects the user's device type (e.g., recognizing an iPhone) and shows device-specific payment options (Apple Pay), which simplifies the payment flow and keeps the user focused on a single call-to-action rather than hunting through generic payment methods. The case study notes personalization is "double-edged" — it can also degrade an experience if the underlying assumption is wrong, so any personalization should be tested rather than assumed to always help.

### 4. Reducing Decision Load (Amazon's 1-Click Default)
When a screen presents multiple options with none pre-selected, it forces the user into an extra decision before they can proceed — the screen is "invalid by default." The core principle is to reduce the number of decisions a user must make and to only ask for input when it's absolutely necessary. Uber Eats is cited as a positive example: its tip screen already has a default amount selected, so the user can simply tap through instead of deliberating. Signal's donation-amount screen lacks this default, leaving the amount field unselected and forcing deliberation. The suggested fix is to pre-select the "most popular" donation amount by default, removing that decision burden while still letting the user change it if they want.

### 5. Social Proof
The case study recommends reassuring users that they are not alone in making the decision to donate — showing some form of social proof (e.g., a running count of supporters, or a note like "joined by X supporters") can help "tip the scale" for someone who is on the fence about whether contributing is worthwhile or normal behavior among Signal's user base.

### 6. Peak-End Rule
People judge an experience largely by how it ends, and if a product wants to encourage a behavior to be repeated, making the end of that experience memorable is one of the most effective levers. After a user completes a donation via Apple Pay, Signal shows no custom confirmation screen — just a generic payment-completion result, with no acknowledgment of the contribution or the user's role in supporting the mission. The case study suggests replacing this with a "heart-warming" confirmation screen that conveys genuine human empathy and thanks, making the moment memorable enough to encourage the user to donate again in the future.

## Key Takeaways
- Stating the benefit and rationale ("why") for an ask converts better than stating only the action ("what").
- Placement matters: prompts positioned in banner-blind zones (screen edges/bottom) get ignored regardless of how good the message is.
- Device-aware personalization (e.g., surfacing Apple Pay to iPhone users) is a legitimate, low-friction way to streamline payment flows — but personalization assumptions should still be tested.
- Removing unnecessary decisions (e.g., pre-selecting a default donation amount) reduces drop-off at the point of commitment.
- Social proof can reassure hesitant users that contributing is a normal, validated action.
- Endings deserve as much design attention as the ask itself — a generic confirmation wastes an opportunity to build a memorable, repeatable positive association with the brand.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to donation/support flows, freemium-to-paid upgrade prompts, in-app payment or checkout screens, and any "ask" moment (e.g., a soft paywall, a tipping prompt, or a review/referral request) where a company wants to grow revenue or engagement without relying on manipulative dark patterns. It's also a good reference for confirmation/success screens that follow a monetary or effortful action.
