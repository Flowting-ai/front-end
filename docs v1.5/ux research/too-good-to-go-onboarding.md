# 3 UX Tips To Make "Aha Moments" Click

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/too-good-to-go-onboarding

## Overview
This case study looks at the onboarding flow of Too Good To Go, an app that lets users buy discounted "surprise bags" of unsold food from local businesses to reduce food waste. The article is framed around a first-person walkthrough of a new user's onboarding journey, identifying that the app's "aha moment" — the point where a user truly understands the product's value — arrives too late, only after the user has already reserved food and stumbled onto the waste-reduction mission. The case study is a useful reference for onboarding flows in multi-sided marketplace apps (here: consumers, participating businesses, and an environmental mission), where the core value proposition risks getting buried under feature explanations and generic permission requests.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Aha Moment (and the "How vs. Why" problem)
The "aha moment" is defined as the moment when a person first realizes the real value of a product. Too Good To Go's original onboarding starts by explaining *how* the app works (its mechanics/features) rather than *why* it matters (the value proposition — saving money, saving good food from being wasted, helping the environment). Because the flow leads with mechanics, the emotionally resonant value proposition doesn't land until much later, after the user has already reserved a "surprise bag" and encountered the environmental mission organically. The recommended fix is a **How → Why shift**: restructure the opening screens to foreground the value proposition (cost savings, environmental/waste-reduction impact) immediately, before explaining the mechanics of how the app functions. Leading with "why" is intended to accelerate the time-to-aha-moment so users grasp the product's significance from the very first screens rather than discovering it later.

### 2. Tell → Show Principle
The case study observes that the app's "surprise bags" — its core product/inventory concept — are referenced only vaguely in the onboarding copy, without a clear visual representation of what a surprise bag actually looks or feels like. This vagueness leaves new users uncertain about what they're actually signing up to receive. The recommended fix is to move from *telling* users about surprise bags in text to *showing* them concretely — for example, using a short video or clear visual demonstration that walks through what receiving and opening a surprise bag actually looks like in practice. Showing rather than describing reduces ambiguity and builds a concrete mental model of the product experience before the user commits.

### 3. Temptation Bundling (Ask → Offer Strategy)
The case study identifies that the app's notification permission request is generic and ambiguous, appearing as a routine system-style ask disconnected from any specific user benefit. It applies the psychological concept of **Temptation Bundling** — pairing an "unattractive action" (an action a user 'should do' but has little intrinsic motivation for, like granting notification permissions) with an "attractive reward" (something the user genuinely 'wants'). Concretely, the recommendation is to replace a generic ask like "Enable our notifications" with a benefit-specific framing such as "Don't miss your surprise bag" — directly tying the permission grant to a tangible, desirable outcome (not missing out on food they want). This is called an **Ask → Offer Strategy**: shifting from asking for permission in the abstract to offering a concrete benefit that happens to require that permission.

## Key Takeaways
- Leading onboarding with the value proposition ("why") rather than mechanical explanations ("how") shortens the path to the user's aha moment.
- Abstract or vague descriptions of a core product concept (like "surprise bags") should be replaced with concrete visual demonstrations (show, don't tell) so users know exactly what they're getting.
- Generic permission requests (e.g., for notifications) convert better and feel more ethical when reframed around a specific, desirable benefit to the user rather than a bare system ask.
- Temptation Bundling — pairing a "should do" action with a "want to do" reward — is an ethical way to align user motivation with actions the product needs users to take.
- Products serving multiple stakeholders (in this case: consumers, partner businesses, and an environmental mission) especially need clarity early, since there are several distinct value props competing for the user's attention.
- No specific conversion metrics or A/B test data were cited in this case study — its recommendations are qualitative/heuristic rather than data-validated in the article itself.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to onboarding flows for apps with a non-obvious or novel core product concept (like "surprise bags"), especially where the value proposition involves indirect benefits (cost savings, sustainability/mission-driven impact) that could be more emotionally compelling than a feature list. It's also directly applicable to permission-request moments (notifications, location, etc.) anywhere in a product, where reframing the ask around a specific benefit rather than a generic system prompt can improve both opt-in rates and user goodwill.
