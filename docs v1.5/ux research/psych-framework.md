## Overview

Growth.Design's "Psych Framework" (referred to in some sources as "Psych" or a "psych tank") is a mental model for tracking a user's cumulative level of motivation/excitement as they move through a product journey. It simplifies complex underlying brain processes into something designers can reason about directly: rather than treating a user's willingness to continue as fixed, the framework treats it as a resource that goes up or down with every interaction, based on how much effort the interaction demands and how well it aligns with what the user actually came to do. It's intended to be used both diagnostically (to explain why users drop off at a specific step) and generatively (to identify or communicate new growth/onboarding experiments).

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Psych as a Cumulative, Interaction-Level Resource
Per the related Growth.Design Blinkist case study, which applies this same framework directly: "Every user interaction will either add or subtract Psych, depending on the effort required and the alignment with goals. The sum of these interactions determines how likely your users are to take action." In other words, psych is not a single onboarding-wide score but an accumulating total built up (or drained) step by step. An interaction that demands significant user effort while doing little to advance the user's actual goal drains psych; an interaction that feels easy and clearly moves the user toward what they wanted builds it. [Source: direct fetch of growth.design/case-studies/blinkist-user-onboarding]

### 2. Measuring Excitement on a Simple Scale
According to the web-search summary of the framework's public description, Psych is described as a way to measure a person's level of excitement about an experience, running from zero (no excitement) up to a maximum level (very excited). It is explicitly framed as a simplification/mental model rather than a literal or precise psychological measurement — its value is in making an otherwise invisible, internal state (motivation) into something a design team can discuss and design against concretely. [Source: web search summary, not independently verified against the primary article]

### 3. Visualizing the Journey with a "Psych Tank"
The web-search summary describes a "psych tank" visualization as one way to apply the framework: tracking a user's psych level as it rises and falls over the course of a journey, similar to a fuel or resource gauge, so that ups and downs across specific screens or steps become visible at a glance. This is described as useful for re-thinking not just first-time onboarding but also how returning/repeat users feel each time they re-enter the product. [Source: web search summary, not independently verified against the primary article]

### 4. Applying Psych to Diagnose a Real Flow (Blinkist Example)
The Blinkist case study (a separate, currently-accessible Growth.Design article that uses this same framework) shows the framework applied concretely: it critiques Blinkist's onboarding as systematically draining the user's psych through "a series of optional questions that provided very little value in return," along with requesting tracking permissions immediately, imposing a daily-habit setup step without explaining its purpose, and ultimately delivering weak personalization results despite having asked the user several questions up front. Each of these steps demanded user effort (answering questions, granting permissions) without a clear, immediate payoff — draining psych rather than building it. That same article also ties the Psych framework to several companion principles: progressive disclosure (avoiding overwhelming users with features too early), framing & anchoring (presenting information in a way aligned with the user's own goals), the endowment effect (making users value the selections/inputs they've already made), and honoring personalization expectations (following through on what customization was promised). [Source: direct fetch of growth.design/case-studies/blinkist-user-onboarding]

## Key Takeaways
- Psych/excitement is best treated as a running total that accumulates or depletes with every single interaction, not a one-time impression formed at the start of a journey.
- Any interaction that asks for user effort without a clear, aligned payoff should be treated as a psych-draining step and scrutinized.
- Visualizing psych over the course of a journey (e.g., a rising/falling "tank") can make invisible motivation drop-off visible and discussable across a team.
- The framework is meant to apply beyond first-time onboarding — returning-user experience should be evaluated with the same lens.
- Related principles (progressive disclosure, framing/anchoring, endowment effect, personalization follow-through) are typically discussed alongside the Psych framework as concrete levers for protecting or building psych.

## Applicability Notes
This framework is most relevant to onboarding flow design and any multi-step flow (setup wizards, signup, first-run experiences) where teams need a shared way to reason about *why* users drop off at a specific step. It's also applicable to returning-user experience audits and to prioritizing which friction points in a flow are worth fixing first, since steps that both demand high effort and offer low goal-alignment are the clearest candidates for redesign.
