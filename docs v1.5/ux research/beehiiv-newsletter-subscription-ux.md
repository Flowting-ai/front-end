# Beehiiv subscription: 5 small UX mistakes that make a BIG difference

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/beehiiv-newsletter-subscription-ux

## Overview
This case study traces a user's journey after discovering a designer's post on Twitter/X, subscribing to their Beehiiv-powered newsletter, and then hitting a confusing recommendation/premium-upsell sequence immediately after subscribing. It catalogs five distinct small UX mistakes in that post-subscription flow and then shows how each one can be fixed by applying specific, well-known psychology/UX principles. It's a useful reference for post-signup upsell and recommendation flows, where seemingly minor sequencing and copy decisions compound into real confusion and lost conversion.

## The 5 UX Mistakes Identified

### 1. Generic Copy
The confirmation/recommendation flow uses impersonal, generic language that reads as algorithmically generated rather than a personal endorsement from the newsletter creator the user just subscribed to. Because the copy doesn't reference the creator by name or voice, the case study notes that as a direct consequence, "Soren [the creator] is already out of mind" — the personal connection that got the user to subscribe in the first place evaporates the moment the flow becomes generic.

### 2. Excessive Options
Immediately after subscribing, the user is presented with too many additional newsletter recommendations at once. The case study describes the effect of scanning through this list: "I had time to imagine 7 other newsletters making me" lose focus — meaning the sheer number of competing options pulled attention away from any single, meaningful next step and diluted the user's engagement.

### 3. Poor Perceived Value Presentation
The premium subscription offer is presented as a mostly empty table with minimal supporting details, failing to communicate why it's worth paying for. The case study characterizes this as showing "one item without more details—falls flat," meaning the offer lacks the visual and informational richness needed to make its value legible at a glance.

### 4. Confusing Information Hierarchy
Multiple unfamiliar features and offers are surfaced simultaneously without any explanatory context, leaving the user unsure what's being asked of them. The user's reaction captures the confusion directly: "Do I have to pay...What's the extra idea...And why is this empty?" — three separate open questions triggered by a single screen, indicating the hierarchy and framing failed to answer basic orientation questions before asking for a decision.

### 5. Flow Sequencing Issues
The flow allows (or encourages) users to explore alternative newsletters before they've completed engaging with the primary offer (the premium upsell from the newsletter they just subscribed to). The case study notes this ordering "creates even more confusion," since it interrupts what should be a single, focused decision with a detour into unrelated options before that first decision is resolved.

## Psychology Principles Applied in the Improved Redesign

### A. Singularity Effect & Halo Effect
The redesign puts the newsletter creator front and center as the authoritative, recognizable voice behind the premium offer, rather than presenting it as a generic platform-level upsell. This leans on the halo effect (trust/goodwill toward the creator transfers to the offer) combined with the singularity effect (focusing attention on one distinct entity rather than a diffuse list).

### B. Power of "Free"
The word "free" is used deliberately in the copy because it has an outsized positive effect on decision-making compared to other pricing language, even when the underlying value proposition is otherwise the same.

### C. Visual Anchors
Graphic/visual elements are added to guide the user's attention through the offer and reduce the cognitive load of parsing an otherwise text-heavy or empty-looking screen — directly addressing Mistake #3 (poor perceived value presentation).

### D. Reducing Hick's Law (Choice Overload)
Hick's Law states that the time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available. The redesign directly addresses Mistake #2 by capping the number of alternative newsletter recommendations shown to just 3, which decreases decision paralysis compared to the original longer list.

### E. Framing Effect
The premium offer is recontextualized in the improved copy as "the best idea of the week" — a framing change that shifts how the same underlying offer is perceived, making it feel like a timely, curated highlight rather than a generic upsell prompt.

### F. Recognition Over Recall
The principle is stated directly in the case study: "It's easier for people to recognize something shown on screen than to recall it from memory." The redesign leans on this by keeping visual reminders of the creator/context on screen rather than expecting the user to remember why they subscribed or who Soren is by the time they reach the offer screen.

## Additional Insight on Flow Sequencing
The case study draws a broader lesson directly tied to Mistake #5: "A great way to eliminate potential pitfalls in a journey is to re-order or shorten your customer's flow." This reflects that some UX problems aren't fixable purely through better copy or visuals — sometimes the sequence of steps itself needs to change to prevent confusion from ever arising.

## Metrics & Data
No conversion percentages or quantified metrics are provided in the case study — the analysis is entirely qualitative, based on the narrated first-person user experience and the named psychology/UX principles used to redesign the flow.

## Key Takeaways
- Post-signup upsell copy that loses the personal voice of the creator/brand the user just engaged with quickly becomes forgettable ("out of mind").
- Too many simultaneous options at a decision point (Hick's Law) dilutes focus even when each individual option is reasonable on its own.
- An offer's perceived value depends heavily on how visually and informationally rich its presentation is — an empty-looking table undersells even a genuinely good offer.
- Screens that raise more questions than they answer ("do I have to pay?", "what's this?") indicate a hierarchy/context failure, not just a copy failure.
- The order of steps in a flow matters as much as the content of any single step — sometimes the fix is re-sequencing rather than rewriting.
- Small, well-known principles (halo effect, framing, recognition over recall, power of "free") can be combined to fix a flow without needing a full redesign.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to post-signup/post-subscription upsell screens, newsletter or content-recommendation flows, and any confirmation step that tries to cross-sell or upsell immediately after a primary conversion action. It's also broadly useful for choice-overload problems (recommendation lists, comparison tables) and for evaluating whether upsell copy preserves the trust/relationship established earlier in the funnel.
