# How to avoid (and repair) these 3 critical design blunders

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/design-blunders

## Overview
The case study is framed around a single bad experience: a user browsing blue-light glasses is interrupted by a poorly designed email opt-in pop-up that derails the shopping experience through visual chaos and confusing messaging. Rather than being about one product's full journey, this case study is a focused teardown of a single high-friction UI moment (a marketing pop-up), used to illustrate three recurring, generalizable visual-design mistakes and how to repair each one. It's a useful reference for anyone auditing pop-ups, modals, banners, or any lightweight conversion surface where visual design quality directly determines whether the message gets received or dismissed on sight.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Lack of Contrast
The problem: when foreground elements (text, CTA button, key message) don't stand out clearly from the background, they compete visually instead of one leading the eye to the other — the case study describes the original pop-up's call-to-action as "nearly invisible" as a result. The repair applied was to simplify the background itself (removing competing visual noise behind the content) so that a clear separation opens up between the backdrop and the primary message, letting the CTA read immediately rather than getting lost. The broader principle drawn from this: "anytime you want visitors to focus on a specific question or task, use visual contrast to your advantage" — contrast isn't just an aesthetic nicety, it's the mechanism that tells the eye where to look first.

### 2. Missing Visual Hierarchy
The problem: in the original pop-up, "everything pulls your attention" at once — logotypes, all-caps headline text, multiple competing photos, and more than one call-to-action button are all fighting for the same moment of user attention, so nothing actually wins and the user's eye has nowhere obvious to land. The repair involved expanding the pop-up's canvas (giving elements room to breathe) and reducing the number of visually competing elements, deliberately establishing one clear focal point through systematic prioritization of what matters most (the offer and the single CTA) over secondary elements (logos, decorative imagery).

### 3. Poor Readability
The problem: the original pop-up combined white text laid directly over a busy image with black text inside separate boxes, and this mixed-contrast, mixed-context text treatment severely hurt legibility — users have to work to parse text that keeps changing contrast and background as they read. The repair was to increase spacing between elements, eliminate any situation where text sits directly on top of a photographic image, and apply cleaner, more consistent typography, all in service of making the copy scannable at a glance rather than requiring active effort to decode.

## Supporting Design/Copy Principles Applied in the Repair
- **Concise copywriting:** the rewritten pop-up cut down wordiness substantially. The case study notes explicitly that "it's a lot harder to explain that same concept with fewer words," acknowledging that concise copy takes more work to produce but yields a meaningfully better result in a high-friction UI moment like a pop-up.
- **Tone refinement:** the redesign eliminated all-caps "shouting" text and removed subtly shaming copy (an example cited: language like "I prefer to pay in full," which frames the alternative choice as a judgment on the user), replacing it with benefit-focused messaging that doesn't make the user feel criticized for their choice.
- **Priming via product imagery:** the redesign added an actual image of the product category being browsed (the glasses) alongside the discount offer, using visual association between the offer and the real product to strengthen recall and make the discount feel concretely tied to what the user was already looking at, rather than existing as an abstract, generic promotion.

## Metrics Cited
- The case study cites that "design influences about 80 percent of buying decisions and user satisfaction," used to argue that visual-design quality isn't cosmetic — it's a primary driver of commercial outcomes, and getting it wrong (as in this pop-up) represents, in the article's words, "a sure loss."

## Key Takeaways
- Contrast is a directional tool: use it deliberately to tell the user's eye exactly where to look for the one thing that matters most in that moment.
- If multiple elements are all vying for attention with equal visual weight, none of them win — establish one clear hierarchy and let secondary elements recede.
- Never require the reader to parse text against a busy or inconsistent background (image + boxed text mixed together); prioritize plain, high-contrast, well-spaced text for scanability.
- Shorter, more deliberate copy almost always outperforms a wordier explanation in high-friction UI moments like pop-ups, even though it's harder to write.
- Avoid guilt- or shame-adjacent copy on decline/alternative options — frame choices neutrally or positively rather than making the user feel judged.
- Tying an offer to a real, relevant product image (priming) makes a promotion feel concrete and specific rather than generic.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most directly relevant to any lightweight, interruption-style conversion surface — email opt-in pop-ups, exit-intent modals, promotional banners, cookie/consent overlays — where the entire success of the interaction depends on the user parsing the message correctly within a second or two. The underlying contrast/hierarchy/readability lessons, however, generalize to any UI surface (forms, landing pages, cards) where multiple elements compete for attention and legibility is at risk.
