# The Psychology Behind McDonald's $2 Billion Self-Serve Kiosks

**Source:** https://growth.design/case-studies/mcdonalds-self-serve-ux

## Overview
This case study follows a first-person narrative of a designer ordering food at a McDonald's self-serve kiosk in London, documenting the psychological tactics embedded throughout the ordering journey — from the entry screen through menu navigation, upselling, add-ons, and checkout. The story moves from app-promotion prompts, to menu framing, to upsell/anchoring mechanics, to impulse add-ons, ending in a reflection on the ethics of using persuasive design to maximize basket size. It's a useful, if ethically double-edged, reference for self-serve ordering/checkout flows and for understanding how sequential psychological nudges compound across a single transaction.

## Principles & Tactics

### 1. Tracking & Retention via App Promotion
The kiosk repeatedly surfaces app-promotion touchpoints throughout the ordering experience — buttons like "Redeem points" and "Order & earn" appear at multiple points in the flow, not just once at the start. The underlying goal is customer data collection: getting users into the loyalty app lets McDonald's track individual spending habits and use that data to personalize future offers, driving longer-term engagement beyond the single transaction. The case study notes this promotion happens "throughout the entire kiosk experience" rather than being confined to a single screen.

### 2. Jobs-To-Be-Done (JTBD) Shortcuts
Rather than dropping users straight into one large, undifferentiated menu, the kiosk's entry screen splits options into four goal-oriented categories that map to a customer's actual reason for visiting: "I want a burger...", "I want something healthier...", "I'm eating with friends...", and "I just need a drink...". This JTBD-based framing reduces decision friction by letting users self-select into a relevant subset of the menu immediately, rather than scanning the entire catalog. The case study connects this to Hick's Law — the principle that more available options leads to slower decision-making — so segmenting the menu by intent shortcuts that slowdown.

### 3. System 1 Thinking via "Popular Picks"
The kiosk surfaces "Popular picks" — pre-selected, data-backed combinations of items — displayed with "juicy," appetizing photos rather than a plain list. This nudges customers toward fast, instinctive (System 1) choices instead of deliberately comparing every option analytically. By curating defaults visually rather than presenting a neutral list, the interface reduces the cognitive effort of choosing and steers customers toward products McDonald's wants to move.

### 4. Sensory Appeal
Kiosk screens are described as "packed with loads of images" of food, with large, appetizing burger photography dominating the visual hierarchy. Legally required calorie information is present but rendered as small "fine print," easy to skip over. The designer's own experience is quoted directly: "My brain completely ignored the calories" — illustrating how strong visual/sensory stimuli can suppress a user's engagement with adjacent informational text, even when that information is technically visible.

### 5. Framing (selective information architecture)
When presenting meal-size upgrade choices, the kiosk's screens omit calorie information from the comparison entirely, framing the decision purely around "the small price difference between meal sizes." By stripping out the health/calorie dimension and leaving only a minor price delta visible, the interface directs the customer's attention away from the larger health impact of upsizing. The case study notes this same omission pattern — "Still no calories!" — recurs across multiple upsell screens, not just one.

### 6. Decoy Effect / Anchoring via Default Sizing
For meal upgrades, the kiosk offers only "Medium" and "Large" as size choices — no "Small" option is presented. Without a smaller anchor to compare against, "Large" no longer feels like an excessive choice; it feels like the reasonable/default option. The narrative captures this with the resigned reaction "Large it is... {Sigh}", and the case study explicitly contrasts how much bigger "Large" *feels* when a "Small" option exists elsewhere in a customer's mental frame of reference versus when it's simply presented as one of only two options.

### 7. Cognitive Scarcity via a Hidden Order Summary
The add-on screen — described as having a "supermarket pre-checkout aisle feel" — does not display a running summary of what's already been ordered. The case study states the underlying mechanism explicitly: "you're more likely to buy more if you can't recall how much you ordered." Impulse items like cheesy bread dippers are promoted on this screen without any visible reminder of the order's current contents or total, and the narrator ends up adding more items despite having intended to stop.

### 8. Removal of Social Judgment (Shame Reduction)
By removing a human cashier from the transaction, the kiosk eliminates the social pressure/judgment a customer might feel when ordering a large or indulgent quantity of food in front of another person. Without a human witness, customers report feeling freer to order more than they otherwise might. The case study ties this design choice to a concrete outcome metric: self-serve kiosks "increased their revenue by 15%" overall.

## Key Metrics & Data Cited
- **$2 billion** — figure referenced in the article's title as McDonald's investment in the kiosk rollout.
- **15%** — revenue increase attributed to the self-serve kiosks.
- The case study is presented as roughly a 5-minute read/walkthrough.

## Ethical Framing
The case study is explicit that not all of these tactics are ethically equal. It singles out the calorie-hiding pattern (omitting calorie info from size/upsell comparisons) and rates it as "pretty low" on an ethical scale, characterizing it as McDonald's optimizing for "profits over transparency." The author speculates that showing customers what percentage of their daily recommended calorie intake a meal represents would reveal a genuinely "alarming" true impact, and notes it would be "surprising to see McDonald's prioritize health over profits." The piece closes by acknowledging the tension between admiring the kiosk's UX craftsmanship and being uncomfortable with how deliberately it's engineered to maximize basket size, sometimes at the expense of the customer's own stated intentions (e.g., wanting a smaller/healthier order).

## Key Takeaways
- Persuasive tactics compound across a single flow — the kiosk stacks JTBD framing, System 1 defaults, sensory imagery, selective information framing, anchoring via size options, hidden order totals, and removal of social accountability, each adding incremental upsell pressure.
- Removing a smaller option (e.g., "Small") from a size selector is a subtle but powerful anchoring tactic — it reframes what counts as a "normal" choice.
- Hiding a running order total/summary measurably encourages additional impulse purchases, since users lose track of how much they've already committed to.
- Removing human interaction from a transaction can reduce the social/shame friction that normally moderates indulgent choices — a double-edged tool depending on intent.
- Visually de-emphasizing required disclosures (like calorie counts) while amplifying appealing imagery is an ethically questionable pattern — it's technically compliant but designed to be overlooked.
- Not every effective psychological tactic is an ethical one to copy; the case study explicitly flags some as troubling even while explaining why they work.

## Applicability Notes
This case study is most relevant to self-serve ordering, checkout, and upsell flows — particularly multi-step purchase flows with add-ons, size/quantity upgrades, and order summaries. It's also a useful ethical checklist for any flow that uses default selections, anchoring, or hidden totals to influence basket size, prompting a deliberate decision about which of these tactics are appropriate to use versus which cross into manipulation.
